Reducing ISIS SANS data

Once you’ve measured your samples, you cannot immediately publish the data and walk away – life is not that good! A series of steps need to be undertaken so that the data are ready to analyse, and this is true whether you use 1D or 2D data. For ISIS or other sources that use multi-wavelength neutrons, this reduction can be further complex. Luckily for you, our software Mantid can do all of these steps, but it definitely helps to understand what is going on behind the hood! Here, we aim to show you, with some simulated measurements, what is happening in each step of the reduction process.

The reduction pipeline

Select a step to see what it does and how the I(Q) curve changes once it is applied. The simulated data here are from diluted spheres with a 50 Å radius. The Mantid ISIS SANS interface runs these steps in this order during the reduction process. You’ll be able to monitor the reduction for both 1D and 2D datasets, and for those interested in alignment or ordering, there is also a small info section on anisotropy.

More info on time of flight

Time of flight

SANS2D uses a pulsed source, so every neutron arrives at the detector at a time that depends on its wavelength: slow (long wavelength) neutrons arrive later. Each time channel is therefore a different wavelength. Move the slider to flick through the channels, as you would in the Mantid instrument viewer. The sample here is simulated: a single, imperfectly ordered domain of a large hexagonal phase of cylinders, which gives spots at fixed Q.

8 to 100 ms is the event binning range from the Settings tab.

Example spectrum from the pixel that sits on the first-order spot at the chosen time, currently from the beam centre. The pixel follows the spot as you move the slider, so the first-order peak stays under the marker and the other peaks are the higher-order spots crossing that same pixel.

Detector image, beam centre and mask

This is a simulated rear-detector image (log intensity). Drag the cross to move your beam centre, and change the beam-stop mask. The radial average is taken about the cross. A wrong centre smears the sphere oscillations.

White circle: mask. Cross: your centre. Dot: true centre.

Q binning

Radial averaging sorts pixels into Q bins. Fewer, wider bins reduce noise but lose resolution. Log spacing keeps bins wide at high Q, where counts are low.

Multiple scattering

Longer wavelengths are scattered more strongly, so a neutron is more likely to scatter twice in the sample. The reduction assumes each neutron scatters once. Tick any wavelengths from 1 to 16 Å to overlay them, and raise the scattering strength to see how double and triple scattering fill in the minima and smear the curve. On a time-of-flight instrument every wavelength measures a different slice of Q: long wavelengths reach low Q and short wavelengths reach high Q. A sphere of radius 50 Å is used, and a small flat background (0.003 cm⁻¹) is added to every curve, as in a real measurement, so nothing falls to zero.

Scattering strength grows as wavelength squared, which is why the effect is much worse at 16 Å than at 4 Å. It also grows with sample thickness and concentration.

The Mantid reduction interface

This is the ISIS SANS reduction window in Mantid (Interfaces, then SANS, then ISIS SANS). Hover over or tap any part of it to see what it does. Click Settings, then the sub-tabs along its top, to explore the settings, or click Sum Runs to see how runs are added together. Click Runs to go back.
