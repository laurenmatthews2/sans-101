Guinier analysis

A simple form of analysis is Guinier analysis coming from a key scientist Andre Guinier, who literally wrote the book on small-angle scattering. He also developed an equation to describe the low-q region of data, giving a simple way of calculating the radius of gyration. For an object of a discrete size, the intensity should follow: lnI = lnI(0) – q2Rg2/3. This only falls within the Guinier valid region, where q·Rg < 1.3. Have a look at the example dataset, uploaded in the visualise data section, and see whether you can apply a Guinier analysis to it.

Porod analysis

Another key diagnostic tool is Porod analysis, developed by the Austrian physicist Georges Porod. While Guinier looks at overall particle size at low-q, Porod analysis focuses on the high-q asymptotic region to yield insights into specific surface areas, particle morphology, and the sharpness of interfacial boundaries. For a system with smooth, sharp interfaces, the scattering intensity follows Porod’s law: I(q) = K q-4 + background (or I(q) ∝ q-4). This only holds in the high-q valid region, well beyond the Guinier regime, where q·Rg ≫ 1. Have a look at the example dataset, uploaded in the visualise data section, and see whether you can apply a Porod analysis to it.

Form factors

For shape-dependent analysis, there are two important models to follow, one is the description of the particle geometry, and one is the description of interparticle interactions. In this section, we will discuss the description of the shape, also known as the form factor. The form factor describes the scattering from a single, isolated particle, averaged over every orientation. For various geometries, the mathematical description—and consequently the resulting scattering profile—differ, allowing us to pinpoint the assembled nanostructure. A list of the mathematical equations for various form factors can be found in the Sasview documentation (a software for analysing small-angle scattering data). Here, you can explore various form factor models to observe how the curves change with various simple models. When you’ve had your fill of looking at form factors, you can have a go at the challenge! The challenge gives you an example raw small-angle scattering dataset, where you can try to model it – if you get stuck you can also always reveal the answer.

Structure factors

As soon as particles are close enough to feel each other, the scattering is no longer P(q) alone. Interactions correlate their positions, and the measured intensity becomes I(q) = φ V Δρ² P(q) S(q). The structure factor S(q) is 1 when particles are far apart and independent, and it is the part of the curve that carries information about how particles are arranged. Repulsion keeps neighbours apart, which pushes S(q) below 1 at low q and creates a correlation peak near q* ≈ 2π/d, where d is the typical centre-to-centre spacing. Choose between uncharged hard spheres and charged spheres with a screened Coulomb repulsion, then raise the concentration or change the charge and salt and see how the peak responds. Spheres are used throughout, so the lower plot shows how S(q) reshapes the sphere form factor.

Things to try.
  • Hard spheres: raise φ from 0.02 to 0.4. The peak grows, moves to higher q as the spacing shrinks, and S(0) falls.
  • Pin a curve, then change R. The peak position scales with 1/R at fixed φ, because the spacing scales with the particle size.
  • Charged spheres: at φ = 0.05, vary the added salt. Screening shortens the range of the repulsion, so the peak weakens and S(0) rises towards the hard-sphere value.

Guess model

With years of experience, you might be able to spy certain features in your datasets and see a strategy to modelling data. In this section, you can borrow the expert eye to try and find the strategy from features in the data. There are two methods you can follow, one is to try the manual selection. There are four selections to make, which are the: low-q region (q-range: 0.006 – 0.01 Å⁻¹), mid-q region (0.01 – 0.09 Å⁻¹), high-q region (> 0.09 Å⁻¹), and crystalline behaviour (the presence of Bragg peaks). By looking at the plotted dataset, you can select the features you see and get some advice based on those physical observations. You can also try your luck with the automated process, which will try to find those features itself and provide a suggestion for the data analysis strategy – even with your own uploaded data. Just remember this is a simple tool and does not tell you whether something is definitely there or not, it is an advice of a place to start.

Useful Links

Here, we have discussed largely the theory of various data analysis methods, but there is a variety of support to assist you with data analysis. There are options for those who are not confident with computer programming, and other options for those more confident.

  • Sasview: a small-angle scattering data analysis software with a GUI
  • Sasfit: a small-angle scattering data analysis software with a GUI
  • Jscatter: a python package containing form and structure factors for small-angle scattering data
  • ShapeSpyer: a python package bridging computational simulation with experimental small-angle scattering data
  • McSAS: a Monte Carlo approach for extracting form-free size and parameter distributions from small-angle scattering data with a GUI
  • ATSAS: a small-angle scattering data analysis software for biological molecules with a GUI
  • Irena: a small-angle scattering data analysis package for Igor Pro
    • Models are JavaScript implementations of the standard sphere, cylinder and lamellar forms; hard-sphere S(q) uses the Percus-Yevick closure, and the charged S(q) is a rescaled MSA (Hansen-Hayter) solved numerically in the browser, so it is not guaranteed to match the sasmodels hayter_msa numbers exactly.
