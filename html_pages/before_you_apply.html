Thinking of applying for beamtime? Start here

A strong SANS proposal shows that you have already characterised your system, that neutrons are the right tool for the question, and that the experiment you propose is realistic on our instruments. This page walks you through what to prepare before you submit.

Before submitting a proposal, work through this checklist:

  1. Talk to a member of the SANS group about your idea
  2. Gather existing characterisation data (DLS, microscopy, SAXS, SEC/HPLC and more)
  3. Work out the scattering length density of your components
  4. Decide whether contrast variation or deuteration could answer your question
  5. Identify the sample environment you will need
  6. Check the q-range you need against the instrument configurations

Talk to us first

You are very welcome to contact the SANS group before you apply. A short conversation can save a lot of time, and is essential if you plan to use a non-standard sample environment, deuterated materials or any equipment that you will bring yourself. Useful things to have ready:

  1. Scientific question: what is your goal and why neutrons are needed to answer it.
  2. Previous knowledge: what do you already know about the sample.
  3. Measurement conditions: temperature, concentration, pH, flow, shear, pressure and so on.
  4. Deuteration: whether the sample is, needs, or could be, isotopically labelled or measured in deuterated solvent.

A SANS (or SAXS) experiment does not see atoms individually; it sees how the scattering length density (SLD) varies from place to place in your sample. Particles, polymers, micelles or membranes are visible because their SLD differs from that of their surroundings. The SLD follows directly from the chemical composition and the mass density, so you can calculate it before you ever come to ISIS, and it tells you how strong the signal will be and whether contrast variation is worth considering.

  • bi is the scattering length of each atom in the molecule (or repeat unit), d the mass density, M the molar mass and NA Avogadro's number.

Why is the chemistry of the system so important for SLDs?

  • Composition matters, including isotopes. Hydrogen and deuterium have very different neutron scattering lengths, which is what makes H/D contrast variation possible.
  • Exchangeable hydrogens (for example in -OH, -NH or -COOH groups) swap with the solvent, so the effective SLD of proteins, DNA, polysaccharides and similar materials depends on the H₂O/D₂O ratio.
  • Density is part of the answer. Use the density of the material in your sample (for a dissolved or hydrated component, the partial specific volume), not necessarily the bulk dry value.

Why are SANS and SAXS complementary techniques?

The answer is in how X-rays and neutrons interact with matter. X-rays interact with electron density, hence, the SLD steadily increasing with atomic number Z. However, neutrons interact with atomic nuclei leading to differences between isotopes, highlighted by a hydrogen (¹H; negative) and deuterium (²H; positive) atom.

Contrast variation: a route worth considering

Neutrons scatter from nuclei, and hydrogen (¹H) and deuterium (²H) scatter very differently. By changing the H/D content of the solvent, or of one component, you can make parts of a multi-component system effectively invisible and highlight the rest. The intensity scales with the square of the contrast, Δρ², which is the difference between the scattering length density (SLD) of a component and that of the solvent.

  • Solvent contrast variation: mix H₂O and D₂O (or equivalent H/D solvents). Cheap, simple, and compatible with sample changers.
  • Contrast matching: choose the mixture where one component has Δρ = 0, so you see only what remains (for example, the protein in a protein-lipid complex).
  • Selective deuteration: label one component (polymer, surfactant, lipid, protein) so it stands out in a hydrogenated or matched background. This needs planning and lead time, so discuss it early.
  • Contrast series: measure several H₂O/D₂O ratios and analyse them together to separate the shape, size and distribution of the components.

Does my size range fit the instruments?

Your DLS, microscopy or SAXS results already tell you roughly which length scales matter. Enter the largest object or correlation length and the smallest feature you want to resolve to see which configurations cover them. This uses the simple rules qmin ≈ π / Dmax and qmax ≈ 2π / dmin, so treat it as a first guide only.
